Research question

How accurately can a diabetes patient's length of stay, number of lab tests, procedures, medications and diagnoses, plus whether they were prescribed a diabetes medication, predict whether they will be readmitted to the hospital?

The problem. When a patient with diabetes leaves the hospital and then has to come back, it is hard on the patient and it is costly for the hospital. Hospitals in the US are even penalized when too many patients are readmitted. If we could flag high risk patients before they go home, staff could focus follow up calls and medication reviews on those patients first.

Why it is important. Which means that better care could go to the people who need it most. In this project we test how far a few simple numbers recorded during the stay can take us, and whether that is good enough to be useful.

Dataset

We used the Diabetes 130-US Hospitals for Years 1999-2008 dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository (Strack et al., 2014, Impact of HbA1c measurement on hospital readmission rates, BioMed Research International, doi.org/10.24432/C5230J). Our copy is a simplified sample of 3,000 hospital stays from 130 US hospitals that was provided for the course. There are no missing values.

Analysis process

First, we split the data into 75% training ( stays) and 25% testing ( stays). Every result below comes from the test stays, which the models never saw during training. Then we trained three classification models: logistic regression as a simple baseline, a decision tree with a depth of 4, and a random forest of 300 trees. Next, we evaluated each one with a confusion matrix, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the ROC curve with the AUROC. Finally, we compared the models and picked a threshold.

Sensitivity is the share of real readmissions the model catches. Specificity is the share of patients who did not come back that the model correctly leaves alone. The threshold is the predicted probability where a patient gets labeled as readmitted. The full code is in the notebook (notebook.py) in the GitHub repository.

Model comparison

This table updates with the threshold slider above.

Which model is best? I chose the random forest because it has the highest AUROC and accuracy. The three models are very close though, with an AUROC around 0.59. Logistic regression is also a good choice because it is simpler and easier to explain. At a threshold of 0.5 every model has very low sensitivity because they mostly guess not readmitted. About 60% of stays are not readmitted, so 60% accuracy is the number to beat.

Where I would set the threshold. Missing a patient who comes back is worse than making an extra follow up call, so I want higher sensitivity. For the random forest, a threshold of 0.40 gave the best balance (sensitivity about 0.66 and specificity about 0.48). Try the slider to see the trade off. Below about 0.35 almost every patient is flagged, and above 0.5 almost nobody is.

Results and conclusion

Answer. These variables do not predict readmission very accurately. Our best model, the random forest, reached an AUROC of about 0.59. A score of 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect, so this is only slightly better than guessing. Its accuracy of 61% at a threshold of 0.5 barely beats always guessing not readmitted, which is 60%. When I lowered the threshold to 0.40, the model caught about two thirds of readmissions, but it also flagged about half of the patients who did not come back.

What this means. The numbers recorded during a stay, like days in the hospital, tests, and medications, are only weakly related to coming back. Things like prior admissions, where the patient goes after discharge, and lab results such as HbA1c would probably help more, but they are not in this simplified file. Because of that, the model could only be a rough screening tool, and it should not be used alone to make decisions about a patient.

Limitations. The sample is only 3,000 stays and we used one random split, so the AUROC could change by about 0.02 with a different split. We also did not tune the models, and the data is from 1999 to 2008.
